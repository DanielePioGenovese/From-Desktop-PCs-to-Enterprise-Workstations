## Foreword: the invisible component

Of all the parts that make up a personal computer, the power supply is the one that receives the least attention and, statistically, causes the most damage. It's an object that doesn't appear in benchmarks, doesn't produce frames per second, doesn't have a Cinebench score, isn't displayed in any RGB-lit showcase (or rather: some manufacturers have tried, with questionable results). It sits at the bottom of the case, often in a separate chamber, covered by a metal shroud, and the average user forgets about it the same day they close the side panel.

Yet every single electron that powers the CPU, GPU, RAM, SSDs, and fans passes through it. The power supply — in English, *Power Supply Unit*, hence the acronym **PSU** which we will use from now on — is the only component that, failing badly, can drag the rest of the system with it. A graphics card that breaks, breaks alone. A PSU that breaks badly can take out the motherboard, CPU, memory, and drives in a fraction of a second, because it's the only thing in the case physically connected to a wall delivering 230 volts of alternating current.

This chapter treats the PSU as it deserves: starting with what it physically does, moving on to how to size it, how to read a label, how to distinguish a good product from a bad product disguised as good, and ending with a repeatable purchasing procedure. As with previous chapters, data that may become outdated (prices, models on the market, generations of graphics cards) are explicitly marked with the **[volatile data]** tag: hardware moves fast, physical principles much less so.

## 1. What the PSU does

### 1.1 AC→DC conversion

The electrical outlet at home, in Italy and throughout Europe, supplies alternating current (**AC**) at a nominal voltage of 230 volts and a frequency of 50 hertz. "Alternating" means that the voltage is not constant: it oscillates sinusoidally, passing fifty times per second from a positive peak value to a negative peak value. This is excellent for transporting energy over long distances — which is why the entire global electrical grid works this way — but it is totally unusable by a digital circuit.

Computer components want direct current (**DC**): a stable, steady voltage that does not oscillate. A transistor inside a CPU has no idea what to do with a 50 Hz sine wave. It wants a fixed 12 volts, or 5, or 3.3, and it wants them stable within a few percentage points, always, without interruption.

The PSU is the machine that performs this translation. The process, greatly simplifying a chain that in reality has dozens of stages, works like this. The alternating current enters and first encounters an **EMI filter** (*ElectroMagnetic Interference*): a group of capacitors and inductors whose task is twofold: to prevent disturbances from the electrical grid from entering the power supply and — equally important and often forgotten — to prevent disturbances generated by the power supply itself (which is a circuit that switches tens of thousands of times per second) from returning to the grid and disturbing other appliances. In inexpensive power supplies, this stage is the first to be sacrificed: components are removed, empty pads are left on the printed circuit board, and the result is a device that, by law, should not even be allowed to bear the CE marking.

After passing the filter, the sine wave encounters a **rectifier bridge**, a group of four diodes that "flips" the negative half-waves, making them positive. At this point, we have a voltage that is always positive but pulses violently: it is not yet direct current; it is a kind of camel hump repeated a hundred times per second. Then the **PFC** (*Power Factor Correction*) stage intervenes, which in modern power supplies is almost always **active** and not passive. The PFC's task is to ensure that the current drawn from the grid is in phase with the voltage and has a waveform as sinusoidal as possible: without it, a power supply would draw current in narrow, violent pulses, polluting the grid and forcing the energy provider to size everything for an apparent power much greater than that actually consumed. A well-designed active PFC brings the power factor above 0.95; it is also the stage that charges the large primary electrolytic capacitor, the big, cylindrical one that is immediately visible when opening a power supply and typically operates around 400 volts DC.

From those 400 volts DC, we need to step down to the 12 volts required by the computer, and this must be done with complete galvanic isolation between the mains and the PC — otherwise touching the case would mean touching the electrical grid. This task falls to the **primary switching stage**, which chops the continuous 400 V into a high-frequency square wave (tens or hundreds of kilohertz), sends it through a transformer, and collects a low-voltage version on the other side. Working at high frequency is the trick that allows modern power supplies to be small: a transformer working at 50 Hz to deliver 850 watts would be a brick weighing several kilograms, while one working at 100 kHz fits in the palm of a hand. The topologies used have names that the reader will encounter in technical reviews — half-bridge, full-bridge, and especially **resonant LLC**, today the de facto standard in the mid-range and high-end because it allows transistors to switch when the current through them is close to zero, drastically reducing losses.

Finally, on the secondary side, the low-voltage but still alternating current is rectified. In quality power supplies, this is done with **synchronous rectification**: instead of diodes (which dissipate a threshold voltage, about 0.4-0.7 V, multiplied by tens of amperes, i.e., tens of watts wasted as heat), actively driven MOSFETs are used, which behave like almost ideal switches. This is one of the main reasons why a Gold or Platinum power supply is more efficient than a Bronze one.

### 1.2 The rails: 12V, 5V, 3.3V (and the ghosts of the past)

The term **rail** indicates a power line at a specific voltage. A modern ATX power supply provides the following voltages to the computer:

| Rail | Voltage | Current Use | Percentage of Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| +12V | 12 volt | CPU (via VRM), GPU, fans, hard drive motors, practically everything | 90-99% |
| +5V | 5 volt | SATA disk control electronics, USB ports, some motherboard circuits | Few watts |
| +3.3V | 3.3 volt | DIMM slots (via regulators), M.2 and PCIe slots (partially), various logic | Few watts |
| +5VSB | 5 volt standby | Keeps the system "alive" when the PC is off: Wake-on-LAN, power button, charging USB | 1-3 A |
| -12V | -12 volt | Historical remnant of RS-232 serial ports. Practically unused today | 0.3 A or less |

The natural question is: why does 12V dominate so overwhelmingly? The answer lies in Ohm's law and power. Power is voltage multiplied by current (P = V × I); for the same power, the higher the voltage, the lower the current. And current is what costs: losses in a conductor are proportional to the *square* of the current (P_lost = R × I²). Transporting 600 watts at 12 volts requires 50 amperes; transporting them at 5 volts would require 120, with almost six times greater losses and much thicker cables. 12 volts is the historical compromise between "high enough to limit current" and "low enough to remain in extra-low safety voltage."

There is, however, a second, more modern and more important reason. No PC component actually uses 12 volts as such. The CPU today operates at around 1.0-1.3 volts; the GPU is in the same range; DDR5 memory operates at 1.1 volts. Those 12 volts are further converted *on-site* by the **VRMs** (*Voltage Regulator Modules*, the voltage regulation modules we discussed extensively in the motherboard chapter) present on the motherboard and graphics card. The VRM is a multi-phase DC-DC converter that takes the 12 volts input and steps it down to the voltage required by the chip, with a precision and response speed that no half-meter long cable could guarantee. In other words: the PSU does not power the CPU. The PSU powers the VRM, which powers the CPU. The PSU delivers "raw" 12-volt power as close as possible to the point of use, and fine conversion happens there.

This architecture has a practical consequence on how power supplies are internally constructed. In older power supplies, designed in the 2000s, the three main voltages were all generated by the transformer with separate windings and "group regulated" (**group regulation**): the control circuit looked at a weighted average of 12V and 5V and tried to keep it within limits. The flaw is evident: if the load is heavily unbalanced — and in 2026 it always is, because 12V takes 95% of the power while 5V and 3.3V are almost unloaded — the voltages diverge. The 12V tends to drop under load, the 3.3V tends to rise, and in pathological cases, it goes outside the ATX ±5% tolerance. The modern solution, adopted by any decent power supply, is **DC-DC**: the transformer generates *only* 12 volts, and the 5V and 3.3V are derived from them via small buck converters placed on dedicated boards. The result is voltage regulation that remains within 1-2% under any load condition. If you read in a technical review that a power supply "uses a group-regulated platform," you are reading — in 2026 — that it is an obsolete design, almost always belonging to the lowest price range.

One last concept: **single rail** versus **multi rail**. Some power supplies virtually divide their 12V into several separate "rails," each with its own overcurrent protection. These are almost never physically distinct rails (there is only one transformer): it is a subdivision made downstream, with separate current sensors on the various cable groups. The theoretical advantage is safety: if a cable shorts, the protection for that single branch trips at a low threshold, instead of waiting for the entire power supply to reach its global threshold. The disadvantage is that with very power-hungry graphics cards, there is a risk of tripping the protection of a single rail while still remaining within the total power. In practice: both architectures are valid if well implemented, a well-protected single rail is perfectly fine for a home PC, and this is a discussion that in the 2010s consumed much more energy in forums than in actual cases.

### 1.3 Why it's the component you DON'T skimp on

We come to the point that makes the entire chapter worthwhile. A shoddy power supply is not "a slightly worse power supply." It is a device that can fail in qualitatively different ways from any other component.

The first risk is excessive **ripple**. Ripple is the residual AC voltage that remains on the DC output: instead of being a flat 12-volt line, the voltage continuously oscillates above and below that value. The ATX standard dictates that ripple should not exceed 120 millivolts peak-to-peak on the 12V rail and 50 mV on the 5V and 3.3V rails. A good power supply stays well below this — 20-40 mV on the 12V is typical for a high-end product. A poor power supply can reach 150-200 mV or more, especially under heavy load. The consequences are neither immediate nor spectacular: the PC works, but the VRMs on the motherboard and graphics card receive dirty voltage, have to work harder to clean it, downstream capacitors heat up and age more quickly, and in extreme cases, seemingly inexplicable instabilities occur — crashes under load, memory errors, random reboots — which the user spends months attributing to RAM, video drivers, or Windows. Ripple is a slow and silent killer, and it does not appear in any advertised specifications.

The second risk is **out-of-tolerance regulation**. If under transient load the 12V drops to 11.2 volts, the graphics card's VRM can go into protection mode or the system might simply shut down. The classic symptom — "the PC only reboots when I play games" — is, in the vast majority of cases, an inadequate power supply, not a faulty GPU.

The third risk is catastrophic: the **lack of functional protections**. A well-designed power supply has a series of monitoring circuits (which we will examine in detail in section 6) that, in case of anomaly, shut everything down in milliseconds. An inexpensive power supply often has these protections only on paper: the supervisory integrated circuit might be a model that supports them, but the passive components that enable them were not installed to save a few cents. In that case, an internal fault can send incorrect voltages to components — 12 volts on the 5V line, for example — leading to instant and irreversible destruction of everything connected. This is not a theoretical risk: independent laboratories that test inexpensive power supplies by deliberately destroying them regularly produce this type of result, and in the real world, there are endless threads of users who have lost their motherboard, CPU, and drives due to a thirty-euro PSU bought on a marketplace.

The fourth risk is the most trivial and most concrete: **fire**. A power supply operates with 230 volts input, with capacitors charged to 400 volts, and dissipates tens of watts as heat. The components separating the primary from the secondary, the filters, fuses, and isolation distances on the printed circuit board are safety elements. In power supplies lacking real certifications — and a CE mark unilaterally affixed by an Asian manufacturer is not a certification, it's a self-declaration — these elements are the first to fail.

Finally, there's an economic consideration that should convince even those who aren't swayed by technical arguments. The power supply is the component with the longest lifespan in the entire system. A CPU

A number illustrates the point. A card with a TBP of 350 watts can exhibit peaks of 600-700 watts over a measurement window of one-tenth of a millisecond. No household instrument sees these: a plug-in meter updates its reading once or twice per second, and on that scale, the peak is invisible, completely lost in the average. But the power supply absolutely sees it, and — crucially — its overcurrent protection circuit sees it too. If the **OCP** (*Over Current Protection*) is set too tightly and its response is fast, the power supply interprets the peak as a fault and shuts down. This led to the phenomenon, which became famous with NVIDIA RTX 30 series cards in 2020-2021 **[volatile data]**, of perfectly honest 750-watt power supplies shutting down PCs with cards whose average consumption was well below that threshold.

This is precisely the problem that the ATX 3.0 standard (section 5) was created to solve. And it's why the correct practical rule is: **take the realistic peak consumption calculated previously and add a 30-40% margin**. Not because the system actually consumes 40% more, but because that margin absorbs transients without triggering protections, and because it places the power supply's typical operating point in the zone where it is most efficient and quiet.

### 2.4 Oversizing: pros and cons

The main **pro** is efficiency. A power supply's efficiency curve is not flat: it's low at very light loads (below 10-15%), rises quickly, peaks around 40-60% of the nominal load, and drops slightly as it approaches 100%. Sizing the power supply so that the system's typical consumption — not the peak, but the *typical* consumption, i.e., what you get when gaming or working — falls within that central window means operating at the point of maximum efficiency.

The second pro is noise, and it's what is most noticeable in daily practice. Practically all decent power supplies today have a **zero-RPM** or semi-passive mode: below a certain load threshold (typically 30-40%), the fan remains off, and the power supply cools by convection. An 850 W power supply in a system that typically draws 300 W will be absolutely silent most of the time. The same system with a 550 W power supply will have the fan constantly running.

The third pro is longevity: a power supply that constantly operates at 90% of its nominal load works with hotter capacitors, and the life of an electrolytic capacitor approximately halves for every 10 °C increase in temperature. The fourth is the possibility of upgrading the graphics card in three years without having to change the power supply as well.

There are two **cons**. The first is obvious: cost. The second is the drop in efficiency at very low loads. A system that consumes 60 watts at idle, powered by a 1200 W unit, is operating at 5% load, well below the point where 80 PLUS certification measures anything. Modern high-quality units still perform well there — this is precisely what Cybenetics certification measures, as we will see — but inexpensive units falter. The practical advice is not to overdo it: installing a 1000 W unit on a system that consumes 300 W peak does not bring benefits, costs more, and improves nothing. The sweet spot is as described above: realistic peak + 30-40%.

## 3. The 80 PLUS Certification

### 3.1 What it actually measures (and what it doesn't)

**80 PLUS** is a voluntary certification program, born in 2004 in the United States, which measures **only one thing**: the efficiency of AC to DC conversion, expressed as the ratio between the power delivered at the output and the power drawn from the wall socket. A power supply that delivers 500 watts to the PC while drawing 555 watts from the socket has an efficiency of 90%: the 55 watts difference have been converted into heat inside the case.

It is crucial to understand what 80 PLUS **does not** measure, because confusion on this point is the source of most incorrect purchases:

It does not measure ripple. It does not measure voltage regulation. It does not measure transient response. It does not measure the presence or calibration of protections. It does not measure the quality of capacitors. It does not measure fan noise. It does not measure *hold-up time* (the time, a minimum of 17 milliseconds according to the ATX standard, for which the power supply must continue to deliver valid voltages after a power outage, to give the system time to manage an orderly shutdown). It does not measure lifespan.

An 80 PLUS Gold label certifies that the power supply is efficient. It says absolutely nothing about whether it is *good*. These are two orthogonal axes, and there are poorly built Gold power supplies and very well-built Bronze power supplies on the market. We'll come back to this in a moment.

A historical note should be added to help calibrate trust: for years, the program was managed by an organization (Clearesult/Ecova) that tested only one sample provided by the manufacturer, without random checks on series production. Various independent tests have found products on the market that did not achieve the efficiency declared on their labels. It is not a fraudulent program, but it is a program with limited control capabilities.

### 3.2 Levels and Percentages

Efficiency thresholds are defined at three (or four) load points. A very important point, almost always ignored: **the thresholds vary depending on the mains voltage**. The "American" 115-volt table, the one that circulates in forums, is not the one that applies in Italy. We operate at 230 volts, and for the same power output, the input current is half, so losses are lower and requirements are stricter. The correct table for Europe is the "230V EU internal" one.

**Minimum Required Efficiency — 230V EU (the table that matters in Italy)**

| Level | 10% Load | 20% Load | 50% Load | 100% Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 80 PLUS (White) | — | 82% | 85% | 82% |
| 80 PLUS Bronze | — | 85% | 88% | 85% |
| 80 PLUS Silver | — | 87% | 90% | 87% |
| 80 PLUS Gold | — | 90% | 92% | 89% |
| 80 PLUS Platinum | — | 92% | 94% | 90% |
| 80 PLUS Titanium | 90% | 94% | 96% | 91% |

**Minimum Required Efficiency — 115V (for comparison, not applicable to the Italian grid)**

| Level | 10% | 20% | 50% | 100% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 80 PLUS (White) | — | 80% | 80% | 80% |
| Bronze | — | 82% | 85% | 82% |
| Silver | — | 85% | 88% | 85% |
| Gold | — | 87% | 90% | 87% |
| Platinum | — | 90% | 92% | 89% |
| Titanium | 90% | 92% | 94% | 90% |

Note that Titanium is the only level that imposes a requirement at 10% load. This is a significant choice: it recognizes that a modern PC spends most of its life in idle or near-idle, and that low-load efficiency has a real impact on electricity bills and heat.

How much is it worth, in monetary terms, to go up a level? Let's do an honest calculation. Suppose a PC that consumes an average of 300 watts output, used four hours a day for 365 days. With a Bronze power supply at 88% efficiency, it draws 341 W from the socket; with a Gold at 92%, it draws 326 W. The difference is 15 watts, for 1460 hours a year, which is about 22 kWh. At an energy price of 0.25 €/kWh **[volatile data]**, we are talking about approximately 5.50 euros per year. The surcharge of a Gold over a Bronze pays for itself in three or four years, not in six months. The correct conclusion is not "Gold is not worth it": it is that **you don't buy a Gold for efficiency itself**, you buy it because the Gold and higher tiers concentrate the best designs, with better components and longer warranties. Efficiency is an indicator correlated with quality, not the reason for purchase.

### 3.3 Cybenetics: The Certification that Measures What Matters

**Cybenetics** is an independent certification body, founded by Aris Mpitziopoulos (one of the most rigorous technical reviewers in the industry), created precisely to fill the gaps of 80 PLUS. It issues two distinct certifications.

The first is **ETA**, which measures efficiency. The methodological difference compared to 80 PLUS is substantial: instead of measuring at three fixed points, Cybenetics calculates a **weighted average across the entire load range**, from 10% to 100%, explicitly including light loads; it also measures the efficiency of the 5-volt standby rail and "vampire power" consumption (i.e., with the PC off but the power supply connected to the mains). Levels range from ETA-A+++ (the highest) down through A++, A+, A, B, C, D, E. A Cybenetics ETA-A unit roughly corresponds to an 80 PLUS Gold/Platinum, but the data is much more representative of real-world behavior.

The second, and for many users the most valuable, is **LAMBDA**: it measures **noise** in an anechoic chamber across the entire load range, with levels ranging from LAMBDA-A++ (average below 15 dB(A), meaning a virtually inaudible power supply) down to LAMBDA-E. It is the only certification in the world that quantifies power supply noise with a repeatable method, and for those building a silent PC, it is worth more than any other data.

Cybenetics also publishes **complete test reports** on its website: ripple, voltage regulation, transient response, hold-up time, efficiency at every load, and protection verification. These are free PDF documents, twenty or thirty pages long, that tell you everything there is to know about a power supply. Consulting the Cybenetics database before a purchase is, today, the single most useful action a buyer can take.

### 3.4 Why a bad Gold can be worse than an excellent Bronze

Now we can close the loop with a concrete and illustrative example. Let's imagine two 650-watt power supplies.

The first is a Gold from a lesser-known brand, built by a third-tier OEM. It achieves 90% efficiency — it did so using low-cost components but with an efficient topology, which is perfectly possible — but it has 85 °C Chinese capacitors on the secondary, a 90 mV ripple on the 12V (within ATX specifications, but at the limit), poorly calibrated OCP protection that trips on GPU transients, a noisy sleeve bearing fan without a zero-RPM mode, and a three-year warranty.

The second is a Bronze from Seasonic or be quiet!, built on a mature platform. It achieves 88% efficiency. It has 105 °C Japanese capacitors, a 30 mV ripple, correctly calibrated and verified protections, an FDB fan with zero-RPM, and a five or seven-year warranty.

The second power supply is, by every metric that matters — stability, quietness, longevity, safety — a better product. It consumes 15 watts more at 650. The lesson, repeated because it is the most important in the chapter: **80 PLUS is a coarse filter, not a selection criterion.** It serves to exclude junk (never buy below Bronze, and practically never buy below Gold in 2026 because there's no reason to), not to choose between two candidates.

## 4. Modularity

### 4.1 Three architectures

**Non-modular**: All cables exit the power supply, permanently soldered or crimped. They are all there, always, including the four SATA connectors you'll never use and the two Molex connectors no one has used since 2012. What isn't used must be hidden somewhere in the case — typically in an empty hard drive cage or behind the back panel, where it obstructs airflow and complicates assembly. The advantage is cost: for the same platform, a non-modular PSU costs 10-20 euros less than a full modular one, because it eliminates the connectors, the connector PCB, and the associated contact resistances. It's a reasonable choice only in the absolute budget range or in builds where cable management is not important.

**Semi-modular**: The cables that are *always* needed, in any configuration — the 24-pin motherboard cable and the 8-pin CPU EPS cable — are fixed; everything else (PCIe, SATA, Molex) is detachable. This is a smart compromise and is the dominant choice in the mid-range. The savings compared to full modular are modest (5-15 euros), and the practical disadvantage is almost nil, except in very small cases where even routing two fixed cables of the wrong length is an annoyance.

**Full modular**: Every single cable, including the 24-pin, is detachable. There are three advantages. The first is cable management: only the necessary cables are connected. The second, less obvious and much appreciated by those who have built PCs in compact cases, is that you can **install the bare power supply in the case and then connect the cables**, instead of having to shove a metal block with a meter and a half of dangling spaghetti into the compartment. The third is the possibility of using custom or "sleeved" cables, for aesthetic reasons or to have custom lengths.

### 4.2 The most important warning in this chapter: cables are not interchangeable

This should be written in bold and read twice, because it is the error that destroys the most hardware related to power supply.

**Modular cables are NOT interchangeable between different brands, and often not even between different models from the same manufacturer.**

The

The operational rules that follow are three, and they do not allow exceptions. First: **use only the cables provided in the box of the power supply unit you are using.** Second: when replacing a power supply, **also replace all cables**, even those that "look the same" and even if the new power supply is of the same brand as the old one. Third: if you purchase third-party cables (CableMod, Corsair Premium Kit, and similar), **verify that they are declared compatible with that specific model**, and not with that brand generically. Serious custom cable manufacturers have configurators that ask for the exact model of the power supply precisely for this reason.

A note on an adjacent area: in 2023, CableMod had to recall its angled adapters for the 12VHPWR connector from the market, after a significant number of overheating and melting cases **[volatile data: historical episode, but the principle remains]**. The moral is not that third-party cables are always dangerous, but that you don't improvise on the high-current path, and that an extra adapter means two extra electrical contacts where parasitic resistances can form.

## 5. Standards and Connectors

### 5.1 ATX 2.x versus ATX 3.0/3.1

The **ATX** (*Advanced Technology eXtended*) standard, introduced by Intel in 1995, defines, among other things, the "Power Supply Design Guide": the document that establishes what voltages a power supply must provide, with what tolerances, with what connectors, and with what behavior under abnormal conditions. The **ATX 2.x** revision, in its various sub-versions, governed the market for almost twenty years.

The **ATX 3.0** revision, published by Intel in 2022, stems from a very specific problem: the transients of modern video cards that we discussed in section 2.2. The central novelty is that ATX 3.0 imposes explicit requirements on the ability to sustain **power excursions** well beyond the nominal power for very short intervals, without the protections intervening and without the voltages going out of tolerance. In practical terms, an ATX 3.0 power supply must withstand excursions up to approximately **200% of its nominal power** for durations in the order of 100 microseconds, with scalar requirements for longer durations, and must tolerate that a single video card connected to the high-power 12-volt connector requests up to **three times** its nominal power for an instant. It is a severe requirement, which in fact forces the designer to oversize the power components and, above all, to **calibrate the protections with a timed response** instead of an instantaneous one: the protection must distinguish between a legitimate 80-microsecond peak and a true short circuit.

The second major contribution of ATX 3.0 is the introduction of the 16-pin **12VHPWR** (*12 Volt High Power*) connector, designed to carry up to 600 watts on a single cable.

The **ATX 3.1** revision, from 2024, does not revolutionize anything but corrects the problem that the 12VHPWR had revealed in the field. It replaces that connector with the **12V-2x6** variant (also known as H++), makes it mandatory for the power supply to be able to deliver the declared power through it, and tightens some requirements on transients. In 2026, a new mid-range or high-end power supply should be ATX 3.1 **[volatile data: the standard evolves]**.

Is

The **12VHPWR / 12V-2x6** is the 16-pin connector: twelve power pins (six 12V and six ground) and four side signal pins, the **sense pins**. The sense pins are the conceptually interesting part: two of them (Sense0 and Sense1) form a two-bit code that the power supply uses to communicate to the graphics card **how much power it is authorized to draw** through that cable — 150, 300, 450, or 600 watts. A card that detects a 150 W code will limit its consumption accordingly. The connector, in its first 12VHPWR incarnation, has had a troubled history: numerous documented cases of housing melting, first with RTX 4090s in 2022-2023 and subsequently with RTX 5090s in 2025 **[volatile data]**. The identified causes were essentially two: incomplete insertions (the connector was not pushed all the way in, contacts only partially touched, contact resistance increased, heat melted the plastic) and current imbalances among the six 12-volt conductors, with a single wire forced to carry current well beyond its capacity. The **12V-2x6** revision addresses the first cause elegantly: **it shortens the sense pins and lengthens the power contacts**, so that if the connector is not fully inserted, the sense pins do not make contact, and the card — not receiving authorization — limits itself to a few watts instead of trying to draw 600 through partial contacts. It does not solve the second cause, which is a design problem on the graphics card side (current balancing), but it is a substantial safety improvement.

The operating rule for the 12V-2x6 is clear: **use the native power supply cable, insert it until it clicks, check that there are no visible gaps between the connector and the card body, do not bend the cable sharply in the first 3-4 centimeters from the connector, and do not use the four-PCIe connector adapter included in the graphics card box if you can avoid it.** Those adapters work, but they introduce additional contacts and are the configuration where most incidents have occurred.

**SATA power** connectors (15-pin, flat, with the unmistakable L-shape) power mechanical drives and 2.5" SSDs. They carry 12V, 5V, and 3.3V. 4-pin **Molex** connectors are an artifact from the eighties, surviving to power old accessories, fan hubs, and pumps: they carry 12V and 5V, are inconvenient to insert and extract, and by 2026 their use is residual.

### 5.3 "PCIe Gen5 ready": what it means (and what it doesn't mean)

This is a marketing term, not a formal standard, and needs to be decoded. On power supply boxes, it usually means two things together: that the unit complies with ATX 3.0 or 3.1 (thus handling transients as per specification) and that it has at least one **native 12V-2x6 connector** wired directly to the power supply, without adapters.

What it *doesn't* mean is equally important: it has nothing to do with the version of the motherboard's PCI Express slot. A power supply doesn't "know" if the slot is Gen4 or Gen5 and doesn't care: it provides 12 volts, period. The term refers to the power connector (sometimes improperly called "PCIe 5.0 power connector") and its ability to sustain power spikes. A power supply not "Gen5 ready" will perfectly power a PCIe 5.0 graphics card that uses traditional 8-pin connectors.

## 6. Internal Quality

### 6.1 The OEMs: Who really builds your power supply

This is the section that separates the informed buyer from everyone else, and the concept to grasp is that **the vast majority of brands that sell power supplies do not manufacture them**. Corsair does not have power supply factories. Neither does be quiet!, nor NZXT, nor Cooler Master, nor MSI, nor — with some nuances — EVGA. What they do is design or commission a product, define its specifications, test it, and put their brand on it, while the detailed electronic design and physical production are entrusted to an **OEM** (*Original Equipment Manufacturer*).

The significant OEMs in the consumer market are relatively few. **Seasonic** is the only one that is simultaneously a top-tier OEM and a brand that sells under its own name; it also produces for others (historically for NZXT, Antec, and others). **Super Flower** is Taiwanese, has an excellent reputation, and is the platform behind many high-end EVGA units and the Leadex brand. **CWT** (*Channel Well Technology*) is huge and produces for Corsair and many others; its quality depends entirely on the platform's tier, ranging from very good to mediocre. **FSP** (*Fortron Source Power*) is another giant, with a catalog ranging from economical to excellent, and also produces under its own brand. **HEC**, **Andyson**, **Sirfa/High Power**, **Solytech**, **Great Wall**, **Gospower**, **Enhance**, **Delta** complete the landscape with very different quality levels. In the lower end of the market, manufacturers like **Sama** or various minor Chinese OEMs provide the platforms for no-name products.

The direct and counterintuitive consequence of this structure is the following: **the same brand can simultaneously sell excellent power supplies and terrible power supplies**, because they are produced by different OEMs on different platforms. A brand's high-end series might be built by Seasonic, and the same brand's budget series by a third-tier OEM, with the same label and logo. **Buying "a Corsair" or "a Cooler Master" means nothing. You need to buy a specific model.**

This, incidentally, is exactly why community tier lists (section 7.2) exist and are so useful: they list *models*, not brands.

### 6.2 Protections

A well-designed power supply includes an integrated supervisor circuit that continuously monitors the outputs and intervenes by shutting everything down in case of an anomaly. The protections that must be present — and whose presence is declared in the technical specifications, but whose *correct calibration* is only verified in independent tests — are as follows.

**OVP** (*Over Voltage Protection*) intervenes if an output voltage rises above a preset threshold (typically around 13.5-14 volts on the 12V rail). This protection literally saves the computer: without it, a fault in the regulation circuit can send destructive voltages to components.

**UVP** (*Under Voltage Protection*) does the opposite: it shuts down the power supply if a voltage drops below a threshold (around 10-10.5 volts on the 12V). This prevents components from operating in brownout conditions, which can cause data corruption on drives.

**OCP** (*Over Current Protection*) monitors the current supplied on each rail (or on each virtual rail, in multi-rail power supplies) and intervenes if it exceeds the threshold. It is the most delicate protection to calibrate, for the reasons seen regarding transients: too tight and the power supply shuts down the PC every time the video card has a peak, too loose and it doesn't protect.

**OPP** (*Over Power Protection*) looks at the *total* power delivered, not the current of a single rail, and intervenes if the system demands more than the power supply can provide. It is the global safety net.

**SCP** (*Short Circuit Protection*) is the most fundamental: it detects a dead short circuit between a rail and ground and shuts down immediately. It must always be present, without exception.

**OTP** (*Over Temperature Protection*) uses an internal sensor, usually on the secondary heatsink, and shuts down the power supply if the internal temperature exceeds a critical threshold. It is the only protection that is often *missing* even in otherwise decent units, and it is the one that matters most if the power supply is mounted with the fan facing down in a case with little space, or if a fan fails.

Finally, there are "ancillary" but non-trivial protections: **SIP** (*Surge and Inrush Protection*) protects against current spikes at startup and mains transients, and **NLO** (*No Load Operation*) allows the power supply to operate safely without a connected load, which is useful during testing.

The practical point: almost all datasheets declare all these acronyms. The declaration costs nothing. What matters is that they are *implemented* and *calibrated*, and this can only be discovered by reading an independent test (Cybenetics, HWBusters, TechPowerUp, Tom's Hardware) where the reviewer deliberately tries to trigger them.

### 6.3 Capacitors, fans, materials

**Capacitors**, along with the fan, are the element that ages. The primary electrolytic capacitor (the large one, around 400 V) and the secondary capacitors work in a hot environment and must maintain their capacity for years. The classic discriminant is the origin: Japanese capacitors — the brands to look for in reviews are **Nippon Chemi-Con**, **Rubycon**, **Nichicon**, **Panasonic** — have better tolerances, lower equivalent series resistance, and, above all, a much longer declared lifespan compared to low-end Taiwanese or Chinese capacitors (Teapo, CapXon, Elite, Su'scon). The other parameter is the **nominal temperature**: **105 °C** capacitors are the quality standard, while 85 °C ones are a sign of cost-cutting. In the secondary, modern quality power supplies widely use **solid polymer capacitors**, which do not have liquid electrolyte, do not dry out, and tolerate heat better.

The **fan** deserves attention because it's the component you hear. The bearing type determines noise and lifespan: a **sleeve bearing** is the cheapest, gets noisier with age, and has a reduced lifespan if mounted horizontally; a **rifle bearing** is an improvement over the sleeve bearing, and is better; a **ball bearing** lasts a long time but is noisier; an **FDB** (Fluid Dynamic Bearing) is now the quality standard: quiet, with a declared lifespan of tens of thousands of hours, and insensitive to mounting orientation. **Magnetic levitation** (maglev) fans are a further evolution, found in the premium segment.

**Zero-RPM mode** (or semi-fanless, or "hybrid mode") deserves a mention. This is the function where the fan remains completely still below a certain load and temperature threshold. The advantage is obvious: a power supply that isn't spinning is a power supply that makes no noise and doesn't draw in dust. The potential disadvantage is that, by remaining still for a long time, the power supply accumulates heat, and when the fan turns on, an annoying on/off cycle can be perceived if the hysteresis is poorly designed. Quality units manage this with wide hysteresis. Many power supplies have a physical switch on the back to disable zero-RPM mode: if your power supply operates in a hot case or near its limit, disabling it is a prudent choice.

### 6.4 Warranty as an indicator

Here's a simple, quantitative criterion that doesn't require reading a single technical review: **the length of the warranty is the best single indicator of the manufacturer's confidence in their product**. A manufacturer offering ten or twelve years is stating, with a contractual commitment that costs them real money, that they expect that unit to function for a decade. No one gives away twelve years of warranty on a product built with substandard capacitors.

| Warranty | What it suggests | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| 2-3 years | Economical product, basic components | Entry / to avoid for serious builds |
| 5 years | Decent product, mid-range | Mid, acceptable |
| 7 years | Good product, quality components | Mid-high |
| 10 years | High-quality product, Japanese capacitors | High-end |
| 12 years | Top of the consumer market | Flagship |

Pay attention to a non-trivial detail: in Italy and Europe, there is a two-year legal guarantee of conformity towards the seller, which is different from the **manufacturer's commercial warranty**. The latter is what matters here, and it is offered directly by the manufacturer, often requiring product registration. Keep your invoice and, if the manufacturer requires it, register the power supply upon purchase.

## 7. Brands and reference lines

This section should be read with a caveat: **manufacturer ranges change every 18-24 months**, and an excellent model can be replaced by a successor built by a different OEM and of lower quality, while retaining the same commercial name. What follows is therefore a map of *historical trends* and *line reputation*, not a price list. **[volatile data: always verify the exact model on an updated tier list before buying]**

### 7.1 Main brands

**Seasonic** is the absolute benchmark for reputation, as it is both an OEM and a brand. The **Prime** line (in Gold, Platinum, Titanium variants, and more recently the PX/TX versions with 12V-2x6 connector) represents the pinnacle of the consumer market, with a twelve-year warranty. The **Focus** line (GX, PX) is the upper-mid range, offering excellent quality/price ratio, with a ten-year warranty. Seasonic's more economical lines (Core, S12) are more ordinary and do not enjoy the same reputation: even with Seasonic, the name on the box is not enough.

**Corsair** has a broad and stratified range, and is the perfect example of why you need to look at the model. The **CX** line is the economic range, historically built by CWT on basic platforms: acceptable but not exciting, with a shorter warranty. The **RM** line, and especially **RMx**, is the core of their offering: Gold units, quiet, well-built, with a ten-year warranty; they are among the most frequently recommended for a mid-to-high-end build. The **HX** and **HXi** lines are a step up (Platinum, digital monitoring in the "i" variant), and **AX/AXi** represents the pinnacle (Titanium). The **SF** line covers the SFX format for compact cases and is considered excellent.

**be quiet!** is a German brand that, as the name suggests, focuses on silence. The **Pure Power** line is the entry-mid: honest, functional, without fireworks. The **Straight Power** line is the upper-mid range and is highly regarded. The **Dark Power** and **Dark Power Pro** lines are the top tier: excellent performance, almost absolute silence, high price. The brand's transversal strength lies in its fans, which are self-produced and among the best in the industry.

**EVGA** has a peculiar history: its high-end units, built on Super Flower platforms (the **G**, **P**, **T** series, and particularly the SuperNOVA G2/G3/G5/G6/G7 and P2/T2), were for years among the most highly recommended. Its economical units (**N1**, **W1**, **BQ**, **BR** series) were built by lower-tier OEMs and range from mediocre to definitely to be avoided. It should be noted that EVGA announced its exit from the graphics card market in 2022 and has progressively reduced its presence in the market **[volatile data: check current product availability and after-sales support]**.

**Cooler Master** is a brand with a very heterogeneous range. The **V** line (V Gold, V Platinum, V SFX) is of excellent quality. The **MWE** lines are entry-level, and their quality has varied significantly between revisions: some are acceptable, others are not. This is a textbook case where the series name is not enough, and the exact revision needs to be known.

**Thermaltake** also has an equally heterogeneous range. The mid-to-high-end **Toughpower GF/PF/GT** line is generally good; the economical lines (Smart, Litepower) are to be avoided for any system with a serious graphics card.

**NZXT** has adopted a simple approach over the years: the **C** series (C750, C850, C1000 Gold and Platinum) is built on Seasonic platforms and enjoys a good reputation. It is an example of a brand that, not having a fallback economic range, has a consistently homogeneous quality.

**MSI** entered the power supply market relatively late. The **MPG A-GF** line (and subsequent ones) is of good quality. Some budget **MAG** lines have received very critical reviews in the past, with cases of units failing safety tests **[volatile data]**. It's another brand that needs to be evaluated model by model.

Then there are brands that have recently gained ground in the high-end market, such as **ASUS** with the ROG Thor and Loki lines, **Super Flower** which also sells under its own brand (Leadex), **XPG/ADATA**, **Lian Li**, **Phanteks**, **FSP** under its own brand, and **Antec** (whose Signature line is very well-regarded). And on the other side, there's the entire universe of brands that shouldn't even be considered: the 30-40 euro units with fanciful wattages printed on the box, sold on general marketplaces, often with names that change every six months. The criterion is brutal but effective: **if you can't find a single independent technical review of that model, don't buy it.**

### 7.2 Community Tier Lists

Since no buyer can read a technical review for each of the three hundred models on the market, the community has produced an exceptionally useful tool: **PSU tier lists**. Historically, the most well-known is the one maintained by the **Cultists Network** (the successor to the old LinusTechTips tier list, which was retired) **[volatile data: tier lists change maintainers and locations over time; search for the current version]**.

The operation is simple: power supplies are cataloged by exact model and wattage, and assigned to a tier (Tier A, B, C, D... or similar schemes) based on available independent tests, warranty return data, and the OEM platform. The practical rule of thumb is: **for a system with a dedicated graphics card, do not go below mid-to-high tiers; for a system with a high-end card, stay in the top tiers.** The lists also explicitly indicate dangerous models, those not to be bought at any price.

Other resources to be aware of are the **Cybenetics database** (which offers the complete reports mentioned), reviews from **HWBusters**, **TechPowerUp**, **Tom's Hardware**, and the destructive tests by **GamersNexus**. All are free, and collectively cover almost all models worth considering.

## 8. How to choose, step by step

### 8.1 The Procedure

**First step: calculate the wattage.** Add the GPU's TBP, the CPU's peak power consumption (PL2 or PPT), and 80-100 watts for everything else. Add a 30-40% margin. Round up to the next commercial increment (typical increments are 550, 650, 750, 850, 1000, 1200, 1600 W).

**Second step: decide on the standard.** If the graphics card uses a 16-pin connector, demand ATX 3.0 or 3.1 with **native 12V-2x6**. If it uses 8-pin PCIe connectors, ATX 3.x is still preferable but not an absolute requirement.

**Third step: set the efficiency threshold.** In 2026, with current availability and prices **[volatile data]**, there's no reason to go below **80 PLUS Gold** for a system with a dedicated graphics card. Bronze is only acceptable in a low-power office build. Platinum and Titanium make sense for those who keep their PC on many hours a day or who seek maximum silence.

**Fourth step — the one nobody does and that makes all the difference: verify the specific model.** Look up the exact model (not the series, the *model with its wattage*) on an updated tier list and, if possible, read the Cybenetics report or a technical review. Five minutes of research at this point are worth more than all the previous steps combined.

**Fifth step: choose modularity.** Full modular if the budget allows and the case is compact or has a window. Semi-modular as a reasonable compromise. Non-modular only for budget builds in large cases.

**Sixth step: check the warranty.** Below five years, in a serious build, it's a red flag. Ten years is what's expected from a mid-to-high-end product.

**Seventh step: verify physical details.** The length of the PSU (1000W and above units can be longer than 160-180mm and might not fit in all cases), the form factor (standard ATX versus **SFX** or **SFX-L** for Mini-ITX cases), the number of actually available PCIe and SATA connectors, and the cable length (in cases with a bottom-mounted PSU and top-mounted EPS connector, a short CPU cable is a serious problem: at least 60-65cm are needed, and in many full tower cases, an extension cable is advisable).

### 8.2 Budget-based recommendations

**[volatile data: prices indicated are orders of magnitude for the Italian market and change frequently]**

In the **entry-level tier** (approximately 60-90 euros, for 450-650W systems), the goal is to buy a decent product without chasing the absolute best. This tier includes lines like Corsair RM (smaller wattages), be quiet! Pure Power, Seasonic Focus GX in lower wattages, MSI MPG A-GF. The advice: aim for a semi-modular 650W Gold unit from a known brand, with at least five years of warranty. This is already enough for a mid-range graphics card, and you can upgrade the GPU later without changing everything.

In the **mid-range tier** (approximately 100-150 euros, for 750-850W systems), the market is crowded, and the choice is wide. Corsair RMx, be quiet! Straight Power, Seasonic Focus GX/PX, NZXT C series, Cooler Master V Gold, ASUS TUF Gaming: these are all competent products. The advice: **850W Gold, full modular, ATX 3.1 with native 12V-2x6, ten years warranty**. This configuration covers 90% of serious gaming builds by 2026, with room for a future graphics card upgrade.

In the **high-end tier** (approximately 180-350 euros, for 1000-1200W and above systems), you're buying something that needs to handle a top-tier graphics card with brutal transients, and you're buying silence and longevity. Seasonic Prime PX/TX, be quiet! Dark Power Pro, Corsair HX/AX, ASUS ROG Thor. The advice: **1000-1200W Platinum or Titanium, full modular, ATX 3.1, twelve years warranty if possible**. If the machine is a workstation that runs hours a day under load, Titanium truly pays off.

A note for those building in **SFF** (*Small Form Factor*, compact cases): the **SFX** and **SFX-L** formats have a smaller market and higher prices for the same power output. Corsair SF, Cooler Master V SFX, Lian Li SP, FSP Dagger are the usual references. Never use an SFX→ATX adapter as an excuse to cram a cheap unit into a small case: quality SFX units have higher power density and thermal sizing designed for that volume, and cheap ones in that format are particularly dangerous.

### 8.3 Common mistakes, explained

**The 30-euro no-name power supply.** This is the cardinal error. A 30-euro power supply advertised as "700W" does not deliver 700 watts: it typically delivers 300-400 real watts before going into protection or failing, has out-of-spec ripple, absent or non-functional protections, low-quality capacitors, and in destructive tests, it's not uncommon for it to fail by catching fire. The buyer's logic — "I spent 1500 euros on components, I'll save on the power supply" — is exactly the opposite of economic rationality. Rule: the power supply should account for between 7% and 12% of the total build budget.

**Cutting it too close with wattage.** Buying a 650W PSU for a system that consumes 620W at peak is technically possible but practically wrong: the power supply will operate at 95% load, with the fan always at maximum, capacitors at their thermal limit, and zero margin for GPU transients. The first sudden shutdown under load will occur within a few weeks.

**Trusting only the 80 PLUS sticker.** We've discussed this at length. Efficiency is not quality.

**Reusing modular cables from the old power supply.** The most destructive error. It's worth re-reading section 4.2. When changing power supplies, you change *all* the cables.

**Daisy-chaining on powerful graphics cards.** Many power supplies provide PCIe cables with **two connectors on the same cable** (one 6+2 in the middle and another 6+2 at the end). The reason is economic: only one PSU-side connector is needed to power two GPU-side connectors. The problem is that those two connectors share the same three 12-volt conductors of the cable, and an 18 AWG conductor is reasonably sized for 7-8 amps. Two 8-pin PCIe connectors should carry up to 300 watts combined, which is 25 amps at 12 volts, distributed over only three wires instead of six: this leads to over 8 amps per wire, with conductor heating and voltage drop. For a card that draws little power (150-180 W), nothing happens. For a card that draws 300 W or more, daisy-chaining is a bad idea, and manufacturers' recommendations are unanimous: **a physically separate PCIe cable for each connector on the graphics card**, drawn from a different power supply outlet. If the power supply doesn't have enough PCIe outlets to do this, it's a sign that it's undersized for that card.

**Using the adapter included with the GPU instead of the native cable.** Adapters from 3× or 4× PCIe to 12VHPWR work, but they add a point of contact and a point of mechanical failure on a path that carries up to 600 watts. If the power supply has a native 12V-2x6 cable, use it. If it doesn't, this is a good argument for changing the power supply.

**Ignore the label's technical specifications.** On a serious power supply, the side label shows the power distribution across the rails. The number to look for is the **combined power on the 12V rail**: on an honest 850W unit, the 12V alone should be able to deliver 830-840W (i.e., about 70 amps). If a "700W" power supply only declares 400-500W on the 12V, with the rest of the power attributed to 5V and 3.3V (which will never use more than 30-50W), that power supply is, functionally, a 450-550W unit sold as 700W. This is a marketing trick typical of budget units and older generations, and reading the label exposes it in ten seconds.

**Buying a used power supply.** Capacitors age, the fan wears out, the warranty is not transferable, and you don't know the conditions it operated under. It's the only PC component for which the used market is categorically inadvisable.

**Forgetting upstream protection.** A power supply, no matter how good, is not a surge protector for the mains. In areas with an unstable electrical grid — and in many parts of Italy, it is — a **surge protector power strip** costs twenty euros, and a **UPS** (Uninterruptible Power Supply) with AVR costs about a hundred euros and protects against voltage drops and outages. For a two-thousand-euro build, it's reasonable insurance. Pay attention to a technical detail: power supplies with active PFC can have problems with inexpensive UPS units that output an approximated square wave during a blackout; for a modern PC, a UPS with a **pure sine wave** output is needed.

## Operational Summary — PSU Decision Checklist

**A. Sizing**
1. Retrieve the **GPU's TBP** from the manufacturer's website (not the retailer's box).
2. Retrieve the **CPU's peak power consumption** (PL2 for Intel, PPT for AMD), not the nominal TDP.
3. Add **80-100W** for the motherboard, RAM, drives, fans, pump.
4. Sum and add a **30-40% margin** for transients and to operate at peak efficiency.
5. Round up to the next commercial size. Verify with an online calculator as a cross-check, not as an oracle.

**B. Standards and Connectors**
6. Graphics card with a **16-pin connector**? → Demand **ATX 3.1 with native 12V-2x6**. No adapters.
7. Count the **actually available PCIe/12V-2x6 connectors**: one for each graphics card connector, on separate cables.
8. Verify there are enough **SATA connectors** for planned drives and an **EPS 8+8** if the motherboard requires it.

**C. Quality**
9. Minimum certification: **80 PLUS Gold** for any system with a dedicated GPU (230V EU table, not 115V).
10. Search for the **exact model** on an updated tier list (Cultists Network or equivalent). If the model doesn't appear anywhere, don't buy it.
11. If available, read the **Cybenetics report**: check ripple, regulation, transient response, protections, and the **LAMBDA** level if silence matters.
12. Verify the presence (and in tests, the functionality) of **OVP, UVP, OCP, OPP, SCP, OTP**.
13. Verify that the label declares a **power on the 12V rail equal to at least 95% of the total nominal power**.

**D. Ergonomics and Physics**
14. Choose **modularity**: full modular if budget and case justify it.
15. Check **unit length** against available space in the case, and **form factor** (ATX / SFX / SFX-L).
16. Check **cable length**, especially the CPU EPS in large cases.
17. Prefer **FDB** fan with **zero-RPM mode**.

**E. Warranty and Protection**
18. **Minimum 5 years**; 10 years is the expected standard in the mid-to-high range. Register the product if required.
19. Provide **upstream protection**: surge protector strip or, better, a **pure sine wave UPS**.

**F. Things Not to Do, Ever**
20. Never reuse **modular cables** from another power supply, not even from the same brand.
21. Never use **daisy-chaining** of PCIe connectors on graphics cards over ~200 W.
22. Never buy a **no-name** power supply or one without independent technical reviews.
23. Never buy a **used** power supply.
24. Never insert the 16-pin connector **partially**: it must click, with no visible gaps, and the cable should not be sharply bent in the first few centimeters.
